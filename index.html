SUBJECT: Atlantis — Myth, Memory, and the Persistence of a Lost World

        
Entry 001 • Classification: Theoretical / Historical Analysis


        

          

        Atlantis is a lost civilisation, cemented in popular culture as a technologically advanced society that sank into the sea and was lost forever. But where did the myth come from? Why have we become so attached to it? What began as a moral, philosophical story about power, virtue and corruption has become an elusive metropolis made of gold. This mythological citadel has evolved from a cautionary tale to a pseudoarchaeological mystery and finally into a prominent location in comics, novels, films and TV shows. But how did we get to this point, and is there any truth behind the story of Atlantis?
The earliest mention of Atlantis comes from Plato, making its first appearance in Timaeus and being continued in Critias. In Timaeus, Plato describes Atlantis as a wealthy empire, demonstrated by his depiction of walls coated in “tin,” “brass,” and “orichalcum”, alongside a temple dedicated to Cleito and Poseidon “surrounded by an enclosure of gold” (Plato, 4 BCE). Plato used the myth of Atlantis to demonstrate how society can fail when it loses virtue in pursuit of imperial power. Atlantis begins as a wealthy, righteous civilisation, but its continued desire for power and abandonment of virtue ultimately leads to its destruction, while the idealised version of Athens remains intact (Morgan, 1998). Plato was no stranger to utilising myths as moral stories, as seen in the Republic. He uses the “Noble Lie” as a means of establishing and maintaining order within his ideal city (Morgan 1998).
Atlantis in the 19th century 
As we continue into the 19th century, we begin to see Atlantis evolve. As the Europeans expanded their presence in other parts of the world, the idea of undiscovered cities and continents became very popular, with authors like Jules Verne featuring Atlantis in his novel Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas in 1869. This rise in popularity made way for theorists to develop a newfound fascination with the elusive Atlanteans. Writer Ignatius Donnelly was among these Atlantis theorists, proposing his own theory in Atlantis: The Antediluvian World (Donnelly, 1882). Donnelly’s theory proposed that Atlantis existed “opposite the mouth of the Mediterranean Sea” (Donnelly, 1882, p. 1), and that “the description of this island given by Plato is not, as has been long supposed, fable, but veritable history” (Donnelly, 1882, p. 1). In his pursuit of scientific evidence, Donnelly turned to the Challenger expedition in 1872, drawing on their observation of volcanic peaks to propose his Atlantis theory (Jack, 2024, pp. 32-33).

Atlantis and Pseudoarcheology
While Donnelly’s theory is easy to dismiss in a modern context, his theory was wildly popular with the press and general public. But why is it a harmful myth to perpetuate? Donnelly did not simply propose the existence of Atlantis as fact, but also attributed the creation of numerous civilisations, such as Egypt, to the Atlanteans, stating that Egypt and its pyramids were built as a recreation of Atlantis by colonisers from the sea. In Lost Cities and the Birth of Urban Archaeology: How Western “Rediscovery” Framed Archaeological Agendas, the idea examined is that European scholars in the 1800s excavated and ‘rediscovered’ what they called ‘lost cities’ when, in actuality, they were known to local peoples (Babou, 2026, p. 2). While Atlantis is not a real city, the harm comes from the erasure of native people from their own histories. The rise of pseudoarchaeological findings from theorists like Donnelly perpetuates a harmful notion that traditional landowners are incapable of producing the structures and monuments of their ancestors.
Atlantis in pop culture
As we move into the 20th and 21st centuries, we see Atlantis changing further. There are films made about it, such as Atlantis: The Lost Empire (Disney, 2001), and characters that originate from it, i.e Namor and Aquaman from Marvel and DC respectively, as well as being mentioned or utilised in numerous sci-fi adventures. It is rare that people truly believe in its existence; however, as we develop as a society, the concept of a utopian, advanced society has changed. The golden temples and walls coated in rare metals have transformed into modern, sci-fi cities with advanced technology, futuristic aesthetics and magic. It has transformed to a point where it is no longer associated with philosophy or pseudoarchaeology but is instead shorthand for a mysterious, technologically advanced city inhabited by often superhuman residents.

Conclusion 
To conclude, Atlantis has served a multitude of political and cultural purposes throughout its history, from Plato’s moral story to the colonial ideas of 19th-century Europe. The belief in Atlantis has never affected its usefulness. While the city and the ideas around it can be used for both philosophical thought problems and for perpetuating problematic, harmful ideas, it has shifted once again for the modern age, transforming into a pop culture figurehead. While it is no longer taken as seriously, it shows that even when Atlantis is no longer believed in, it will remain culturally useful.
