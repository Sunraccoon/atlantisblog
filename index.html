SUBJECT: Atlantis — Myth, Memory, and the Persistence of a Lost World

        
Entry 001 • Classification: Theoretical / Historical Analysis


        

          

        "But afterwards there occurred violent earthquakes and floods; and in a single day and night of misfortune, all your warlike men in a body sank into the earth, and the island of Atlantis in like manner disappeared in the depths of the sea." (Plato, trans. 1929, 25c–d). This is the death of Atlantis as described by Plato in Timaeus: the people of Atlantis succumbing to their corruption and greed, leading to the gods plunging them into the depths of the sea. But while this is Atlantis’s official demise, it continues to live on, shifting from a philosophical teaching into an elusive lost city of gold, hidden at the bottom of the Atlantic Ocean. The fascination with Atlantis persists, but why? Why have we, in pop culture and pseudoscientific spaces alike, found this tale to be so indelible? This essay will examine Atlantis, its evolution into its current state and the enduring issues that it creates. Taking a close look at the manner in which it took on new life in the 19th century, the racist and nationalist ideologies that propelled it and how it has evolved further into a mythical city in comics and children’s cartoons alike.

The earliest known mention of Atlantis is credited to Plato, who first mentions it in his writings Timaeus and continues it in later work Critias. In Timaeus, Plato describes Atlantis as a wealthy empire, shown by walls coated in “tin,” “brass,” and “orichalcum”, along with a temple dedicated to Cleito and Poseidon “surrounded by an enclosure of gold” (Plato, 4 BCE).  It is in Timaeus that he also describes its demise: “ Atlantis in like manner disappeared in the depths of the sea” (Plato). In Critias, Plato describes the greed and corruption of the Atlanteans, stating that “ Human nature got the upper hand; they then, being unable to bear their fortune, behaved unseemly” (Plato). Critias is also the text in which Plato describes the war between Atlantis and Athens, which is where people have drawn parallels between real historical conflicts and the one described by Plato. 

While it is unlikely that Plato was giving a true historical account, it is also possible that Plato was inspired by real conflicts, as it was not unusual for Plato to use metaphors to set moral standards. In the text Republic, written in 375 BC, he uses the “Noble Lie”, in which people are divided in a hierarchical system based on the metal mixed with their souls (Gold, silver, iron and bronze) as a means of establishing and maintaining order within his ideal city (Morgan 1998). It would not be unusual if Plato used the myth of Atlantis to demonstrate how society can fail when it loses virtue in pursuit of imperial power. It demonstrates a clear warning, depicting Atlantis as a wealthy, righteous civilisation, with its continued desire for power and abandonment of virtue ultimately leading to its destruction, while an idealised version of Athens wins the war, retains its morals and remains intact (Morgan, 1998).

This version of Atlantis was widely accepted for centuries; people read and interpreted the work in a multitude of ways, but most consumed it with the concept of a moral tale at hand. However, as we enter the 19th century, attitudes towards the concept of Atlantis begin to change. This change was heralded by Europeans expanding their presence in other parts of the world. The creation of colonies, the East India Trading Route and a general fascination with ‘exotic’ lands. Through this new age of exploration, the idea of undiscovered cities and continents became very popular, with authors like Jules Verne featuring Atlantis in his novel Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea in 1869. 

This rise in popularity made way for theorists and pseudoarchaeologists to develop a newfound fascination with the elusive Atlanteans. Writer Ignatius Donnelly was among these Atlantis theorists, proposing his own theory in Atlantis: The Antediluvian World (Donnelly, 1882). Donnelly’s theory proposed that Atlantis existed “opposite the mouth of the Mediterranean Sea” (Donnelly, 1882, p. 1), and that “the description of this island given by Plato is not, as has been long supposed, fable, but veritable history” (Donnelly, 1882, p. 1). In his pursuit of scientific evidence, Donnelly turned to the Challenger expedition in 1872. (Jack, 2024, pp. 32-33). The Challenger Expedition took deep-sea soundings that confirmed the existence of an underwater mountain range and volcanic peak (Jack, 2024, p. 33). It was this mountain range that Donnelly pointed to as veritable proof of Atlantis’s existence, using this discovery as proof of extensive volcanic activity in the area, powerful enough to sink a continent.  (Jack, 2024). The problems with Donnelly’s ideas do not only arise from his lack of scientific education; when examining the ideas he puts forward, we begin to see an alarming trend. 

While in today’s age Donnelly’s theory is easy to dismiss, at the time his theory was wildly popular with the press and general public. But why is it a harmful myth to perpetuate? The unfortunate side effect of Donnelly’s theories was his attribution of the creation of numerous civilisations, such as Egypt, to the Atlanteans, stating that Egypt and its pyramids were built as a recreation of Atlantis by colonisers from the sea. Donnelly states that “There is no evidence that the civilisation of Egypt was developed in Egypt itself; it must have been transported there from some other country”(Donnelly, 1882, p. 358). This statement is simply not true, but it plays into an enduring idea that peoples from another world, whether that be Atlantis or outer space, truly built the pyramids. This idea put forward by Donnelly is extremely racist and rooted in the normalised colonial practices of the time. 

We see this idea explored in depth in “Lost Cities” and the Birth of Urban Archaeology: Urban Excavations in the Late Ottoman and Early Mandate Middle East and Their Impact on Developing Archaeological Practices by Olympia Bobou, Miriam Kühn, and Rubina Raja, an influential book on the racist views perpetuated by 19th-century archaeologists. Bobou specifically examines the concept that European scholars in the 1800s excavated and ‘rediscovered’ what they called ‘lost cities’ when, in actuality, they were known to local peoples (Babou, 2026, p. 2). By downplaying local knowledge of the cities, European scholars were able to rewrite history for those who were previously unfamiliar. ‘Lost’ cities of the Middle East became tourist attractions in which it was exceedingly popular to collect artifacts and pieces of the ruins to take home, damaging evidence of the culture and history in that area and, in turn, making it even harder to determine if one was not a local (Babou 2026). Alongside the disruption of the sites by foreigners, there was also the denial of the locals being involved in the cities. Similarly to Egypt, many ‘lost’ cities are presented as being built and inhabited by an unknown civilisation rather than by the ancestors of the local people. 

This way of thinking and approaching civilisations is notable when looking at Atlantis. While Atlantis is a fictitious city, the connection that Donnelly draws between it and Egypt is incredibly harmful.
